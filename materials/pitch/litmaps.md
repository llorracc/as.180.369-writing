# What a Litmap is, and why you are being asked to make one

You have signed up for Litmaps. Nobody has yet told you what it is *for*. This
page fixes that, because from class 3 onward it is the tool your literature
review runs on.

## The problem it solves

You have a research question. Somewhere out there are twenty papers you need to
know about, and several hundred thousand you do not.

The obvious move is to search Google Scholar for your keywords. That works
reasonably well and has one serious weakness: **it finds papers that use your
words.** The most important paper for your question may call the same idea
something else entirely — a different subfield's vocabulary, or terminology that
changed in 1997 — and you will never see it. You cannot search for a phrase you
do not know.

## What Litmaps does instead

It follows **citations** rather than words.

You give it one paper you already know is relevant — a *seed*. It then looks at
what that paper cites, what cites it, and what else tends to be cited alongside
it, and builds a map of the papers most strongly connected to yours by those
links.

The point is that **citation links do not care what vocabulary a paper uses.** If
a 1994 paper is important to your question, the people writing about your
question cite it, and it shows up — whatever it happens to call itself. That is
the thing keyword search cannot do for you.

The result is a picture: your papers as a network, usually laid out over time, so
you can see which work is foundational, which is recent, and where the clusters
are.

## What you do with it

1. **Seed it.** Start from one paper you already have — from the JEP issue you
   built, from something in class, from a search. A single good seed is enough.
2. **Look at what comes back**, and add the ones that are genuinely relevant.
   Most will not be. Rejecting papers is the work.
3. **Notice the shape.** A paper everything else points at is probably one you
   have to read. A dense recent cluster is probably a live debate you should
   know about.
4. **Move them into Paperpile.** Litmaps exports BibTeX, and Paperpile has a
   Litmaps integration that lets you save articles straight across. That matters
   because Paperpile is where your `references.bib` comes from, and that file is
   what your paper cites.

## The honest limitations

**Litmaps will not be exhaustive**, and you should not treat a full-looking map
as a finished literature review. It is very good at finding what is connected to
what you already have, and structurally blind to a relevant literature that
nobody in your starting neighbourhood happens to cite. Do some searching outside
it too.

**It is a discovery tool, not a reading list.** A paper on your map is a
candidate. You still have to decide it is worth your attention, and be able to
say why.

## What the free tier gives you

**Two maps, and that is the constraint to plan around.**

The exact limits and prices live in one place, so they can be kept current:
[Services_signups.md#litmaps](../setup/Services_signups.md#litmaps).

What matters for how you work: **two maps is exactly two topics.** Do not burn
one on an experiment you do not care about — and **you are not expected to pay
for Litmaps.** If you hit the limit, tell us rather than buying anything.

## What you are being asked to produce

For class 4 you need **a Litmap for the topic you chose in class 3**, built out
from at least one seed paper, with the papers you judged relevant kept and the
rest discarded.

You will **share your screen and show it** as part of your pitch — as evidence
that you know what has already been written about your question. Be ready to say
which papers matter most **and why**, and why you left others out. That
judgement is what is being assessed, not the size of the map.
